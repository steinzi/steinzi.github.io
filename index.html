The Steinzi pitch, since you asked

I know what happens after the clever demo.

I am at my most useful when the process is messy, the systems are old, and somebody has already said, “couldn’t we just use AI?”

Fifteen-plus years in infrastructure taught me to care about failure, access, operations, and the people on call. Product development taught me to make all of that usable. AI is the newest tool in the box, not an exemption from reality.

01

Find the real problem

Trace the actual process across people, spreadsheets, APIs, ticket queues, and legacy systems before anyone spends six months automating the wrong bit.

Process archaeology, but with fewer little brushes.
02

Build the useful version

Turn the good idea into an operable system: agents, MCP tools, APIs, workflows, tests, approvals, observability, and very clear places where the AI is not allowed to improvise.

The demo is allowed to become infrastructure.
03

Make it safe enough to respect, not trust

Keep credentials out of model context, gate access by session and role, put humans on the consequential decisions, and design failure as a normal operating condition.

Useful autonomy. Adult supervision included.
04

Get humans to use it

Polish the interface, fit the tool to the team, teach the why, document the awkward bits, and listen when the people doing the work say the clever solution is annoying.

Adoption is a feature. Apparently.

The long version, still aggressively abridged

From soldering irons to AI agents.

The through-line was never “networking.” It was taking complicated systems, making them safer and easier to operate, and then convincing humans the new way is less painful.

Act 12010—2016

Hardware first. Consequences included.

Started in electronics, physical security, CCTV, and IT services before moving deep into network operations. The machines were physical, the customers were real, and a bad change could ruin everyone’s afternoon.

Act 22017—2022

Expertise, then a deliberate escape from the box.

Joined Advania as a principal network engineer, became Iceland’s youngest CCIE at the time, taught networking, earned a computer-science degree at night, and learned enough software to stop accepting repetitive work as a law of nature.

Act 32022—2024

Automation became a product, not a folder of scripts.

Moved into lead product development, built cross-vendor automation and source-of-truth systems, replaced legacy tooling, and learned that the hard part is rarely the API. It is the process, the ownership, and Gary’s spreadsheet.

Act 42023—now

AI met the business. Both survived.

Started speaking publicly about generative AI in 2023 and now builds controlled AI automation across infrastructure and business processes: internal MCPs, authorized sessions, human approval, secret-safe tooling, and systems people will actually use.

About

Builder.
Teacher.
Professional skeptic.

I’m a Lead Product Development Engineer at Advania, working where AI, software, operations, legacy systems, and business processes collide — usually at speed.

I started in electronics and physical security, spent years designing and operating business-critical networks, taught networking on the side, and went back to university at night for a BSc in computer science. Along the way came CCIE #60715, CKAD, RHCSA, and a suspicious number of other certifications because apparently I used to enjoy exams.

Now I use models, agents, MCP, APIs, and dependable workflows to remove friction from infrastructure and business processes. Away from the confidential work, I host Network Automagic, founded ISNOG, maintain open-source projects, teach workshops, and occasionally explain that automating a bad process only makes the bad process faster.
